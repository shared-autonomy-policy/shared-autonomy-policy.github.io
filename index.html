Shared autonomy · Policy steering

A joystick is enough to steer a VLA

Pretrained vision-language-action policies are strong manipulators and brittle ones: move a target object 20 cm and success collapses. SAPS mixes a human operator's joystick directly into the policy's action stream — no retraining, no auxiliary dynamics model, no changes to the sampler — and recovers most of what the perturbation took away.

The failure mode nobody trains away

Take π0.5, a capable pretrained VLA, and ask it to put the cream cheese on the plate in LIBERO. It does it. Now slide the cream cheese 20 cm from where the demonstrations put it, leave everything else identical, and ask again. Success rate falls to 22.9%.

The policy has not forgotten how to manipulate. Watch the rollouts and you see something more specific: it reaches confidently for where the object used to be. Across eight perturbed setups, success tracks displacement almost linearly (Pearson r = −0.800, p = 0.005). The low-level skill survives the perturbation; the grounding does not.

LIBERO-PRO makes the point sharper, because it perturbs the task rather than the geometry — swapping which mug goes on which plate, or substituting soup for cream cheese in the instruction. Autonomous π0.5 averages 15.0% across ten such tasks. On three real-world tasks with a DROID-finetuned checkpoint the story repeats: 50% on pick-and-place, 25% on closing a drawer, 5% on opening a cabinet.

The obvious fallback is to take the robot back by hand. That works — a practiced operator on a gamepad completes essentially everything — but it throws away the policy entirely and puts the whole manipulation sequence on a person, which is slow in simulation (limited depth perception from camera views) and tiring everywhere. What we wanted was the cheap thing in between: leave the policy doing the manipulation, and let a person supply only the part it gets wrong.

One line of arithmetic

SAPS does not touch the policy. At each of the 20 control steps per second, the VLA proposes an action and the operator may or may not be pushing the stick. The executed action is a convex combination of the two:

\[\mathbf{a}^{(1:6)}_{\textit{blended}} = \alpha\, \mathbf{a}^{(1:6)}_{\textit{VLA}} + (1 - \alpha)\, \mathbf{a}^{(1:6)}_{\textit{expert}}\]

The first six dimensions are translational and rotational end-effector motion. The gripper is handled separately as \(a^{(7)} = \max(a^{(7)}_{\textit{VLA}}, a^{(7)}_{\textit{expert}})\), so either party can initiate a grasp without the two fighting. When the operator is idle — expert action norm below \(\epsilon = 0.001\) — \(\alpha = 1\) and the policy simply runs.

Everything interesting lives in how you pick \(\alpha\). We compared three arbitrators, chosen to span the range from "the human is a switch" to "the human is a vote":

Takeover
Hard switching. Any detected operator input sets \(\alpha = 0\) and the human has the arm outright; release the stick and the policy resumes.
Blending
A fixed 50/50 mix whenever the operator is active. The simplest continuous arbitrator, and the one we expected people to like.
Cosine
Let the two actions decide. Measure the cosine similarity between the human's commanded direction and the policy's, push it through a logistic (\(\alpha = \sigma(6\cos\theta)\)), and use that as the weight. When the human and the policy agree the arbitrator defers to the policy; when they diverge, authority slides to the human.

Cosine is worth dwelling on, because it gets a useful behaviour for free. It never needs to know what the task is, what the human intends, or how confident the policy is. Disagreement between the two action vectors is itself the signal — if the operator is pushing against the policy, that is the evidence the policy is wrong, and the weight moves accordingly. The formulation also leaves a slot open: any other confidence measure can replace \(\cos\theta\).

Compare that to what the alternatives require. ITPS injects a human-specified position-target gradient into every denoising step of the diffusion sampler. DynaGuide trains a separate dynamics model and a classifier to bias sampling toward desired futures. SAPS needs neither — it sits entirely outside the policy, reading its output and nothing else, which is also why it runs unchanged over both a π0.5 VLA and a diffusion policy.

What the mixing buys

Simulation: most of the gap closes on a fraction of the effort

On the ten out-of-distribution LIBERO-PRO tasks, all three arbitrators recover nearly everything teleoperation gets. Against 15.0% for autonomous π0.5: Blending 92.6%, Takeover 93.2%, Cosine 97.4%, with full teleoperation at 98.8%. Every arbitrator beats the policy alone at p = 9.77×10−4 (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank).

The number we care about more is how much human it took. Blending and Takeover needed operator input on 10.8% and 11.7% of control timesteps, Cosine on 30.0% — against 100% for teleoperation, by definition. Intervention rate did not correlate with task order (p > 0.09), so this is targeted correction rather than an operator gradually taking over.

Nor is the recovery slow. Blending, Cosine and Takeover finish in 11.1 s, 13.0 s and 13.2 s on average, against 30.7 s for autonomous π0.5 — which burns time in failed recovery attempts — and 46.0 s for teleoperation, where a person is flying the whole trajectory through a camera feed. All three are faster than both baselines at p < 0.001.

CALVIN: the simple thing beats the complicated ones

The comparison we find most telling is on CALVIN, steering a baseline diffusion policy across 11 subtasks. Cosine arbitration averages 93% success, against 80% for DynaGuide, 66% for ITPS and 45% for the unguided policy — and it is the only method that stays high everywhere, hitting 100% on all eight articulated subtasks and 70–90% on the three harder block-lift ones.

That could just be "the method with a human in the loop wins." It isn't. ITPS also has a human in the loop, at a comparable rate: 75.5% of timesteps against Cosine's 79.8%, a gap of 4.3 percentage points. Same operator effort, same base policy, 27 points of success between them. What separates them is the arbitration mechanism itself.

The end-effector paths say the same thing from another angle. The unguided policy travels 2–3× farther per episode than Cosine does for the same subtask — undirected exploratory motion that a little coarse directional input suppresses almost entirely.

Hardware: it transfers

On a physical Franka with a DROID-finetuned π0.5, Cosine averages 98.3% across pick-and-place, closing a drawer and opening a cabinet, and Blending 93.3% — against the policy's own 50%, 25% and 5%. Intervention stays partial: roughly 55–69% of timesteps for Cosine and 49–67% for Blending, every one of them a significant reduction against teleoperation's 100% (p < 10−15).

Completion time is the one place the real world diverges from simulation. Both arbitrators beat autonomous π0.5 on all three tasks (p ≤ 0.0212), but against teleoperation they win only on the marker plate and tie on the other two. In simulation teleoperation is slow because the operator is judging depth from a camera; standing in front of the real robot, they simply can see, and manual control gets quick. Shared autonomy's advantage on hardware is not that it is faster than a person — it is that it gets there without the person driving every timestep.

Fourteen people and a gamepad

All of the above was run by two experienced operators, which tells you what the method can do and nothing about what it feels like. So we ran a separate study: 14 able-bodied operators (mean age 24.0, SD 5.8), each completing a LIBERO block and a hardware block under all four conditions, then rating workload on the raw NASA-TLX and force-ranking the methods. One hardware block was dropped for a joint-torque sensor fault, leaving n = 14 in simulation and n = 13 on hardware.

Across that study SAPS reached 8.1× the success rate of fully autonomous execution and 1.8× that of full teleoperation, with active human control on 45% of timesteps, 16% faster completion than teleoperation, and workload down 20.8% in simulation and 28.2% on hardware.

Arbitration method moved workload on both benchmarks (Friedman; LIBERO p < .001, hardware p = .007). In simulation, teleoperation (M = 49.0, SD = 18.6) was more demanding than Cosine (M = 31.1) and Takeover (M = 32.2) — but not than Blending, which at M = 53.1 was numerically worse than driving the robot yourself. On hardware all three arbitrators came in below teleoperation (M = 53.8): Takeover 36.2, Blending 38.5, Cosine 41.2.

The result we did not predict

Then we looked at what people actually preferred, and the two benchmarks disagreed with each other.

In simulation, Blending was ranked first by nobody — zero of 14 — and 10 of 14 operators put it below raw teleoperation. Cosine took 7 first-place votes and Takeover 6. On the physical robot the same arbitrator took 31% of first-place votes, and it was teleoperation that collected none and finished with the worst median rank. The three arbitrators became statistically indistinguishable from one another on hardware (all pairwise pHolm ≥ .65); the whole omnibus effect was carried by teleoperation sitting below Takeover.

Our reading is that 50/50 blending is a bad deal exactly when you cannot see. In simulation the operator is already fighting depth ambiguity through a camera feed; halving their authority means every correction has to be made twice as large and still arrives diluted by a policy that is confidently heading somewhere else. The controller feels like it is arguing with you. Standing in front of the real arm, with real depth perception and a policy whose low-level behaviour is genuinely useful, the same 50/50 mix reads as smoothing rather than resistance — and the workload numbers back that up, with Blending falling from 53.1 in simulation to 38.5 on hardware.

The practical lesson is that the arbitrator is not a detail you tune once. Takeover was the only method that people liked in both settings, and Cosine was the strongest on task metrics almost everywhere — but which one an operator wants depends on how well they can see what the robot is doing.

A single expert user

Separately from the 14-operator pool, one expert robot-arm user with quadriplegia, who uses an adaptive controller daily, completed the study. They are reported as a case study rather than pooled in, on a stated population criterion rather than anything about their results: they saw improved success rates and reduced task times. It is one person and we treat it as such, but assistive teleoperation is the setting where "the policy does the manipulation, the operator supplies the intent" has the most obvious value, and we wanted at least one data point from it.

Where this breaks

SAPS needs a human at test time. That is the whole design, but it means performance rides on operator timing, skill and the interface — everything here used a gamepad, and a different input device could plausibly reorder the arbitrators.

The deeper limitation is that SAPS blends against the policy's action and nothing else. It has no model of task progress, operator intent, policy uncertainty, contact or safety. When the policy is confidently wrong and the human has not noticed yet, nothing in the arbitrator catches it. Cosine and Takeover soften this — both hand authority over as soon as the operator pushes a different direction — but neither anticipates.

And the evaluation is finite: a fixed set of simulated and real tasks, two experienced operators for the benchmark numbers, one embodiment, two policy backbones. Broader LIBERO coverage, more operators driving the quantitative results, more embodiments and more real-world tasks would all sharpen the picture.

Why we think this is worth the space

The result we would defend hardest is the CALVIN one, because it is the cleanest. A convex combination with a cosine-similarity weight — no training, no auxiliary model, no sampler hooks, roughly ten lines wrapped around a policy you already have — beats two methods that each require substantially more machinery, at matched human intervention. That is not a statement about how much a human helps. It is a statement about how much of the difficulty in policy steering is in the arbitration, and how little of it needs to be learned.

The counterweight is the preference reversal. The same arbitrator went from last place to second depending on whether the operator could see the workspace, and no task metric would have told us that. If shared autonomy is going to be deployed with people in the loop — in assistive teleoperation especially — the arbitrator has to be evaluated as an interface, not only as a controller.
